Networks for complex volumes#

In lessons 10 and 11 your network denoised one complex 2D slice. The data you most need a learned reconstruction for are larger: a 3D volume of several contrasts, the subspace coefficients of MR fingerprinting, the frames of a cine. In this tour you train a 3D denoiser on patches of a complex, multi-contrast brain volume, apply it to the whole volume of another subject patch by patch, the way it would run on a scanner GPU too small for the volume, and check that the patch boundaries leave no seams.

Three things change when you go from a slice to such a volume:

  • You denoise the contrasts jointly, as channels of one image, so that the network can use the anatomy they share. Their signal levels differ, so you balance them before the network sees them.

  • The network’s activations for the whole volume do not fit in GPU memory, so you train on patches and apply the network patch by patch.

  • A network applied on a fixed grid of patches leaves seams at the patch boundaries. Offsetting the grid at random and averaging a few calls spreads them out.

Prerequisites: lesson 11, 11. Training an unrolled network.

Learning objectives

  • Build a 3D bartorch.learning.UNet for complex multi-contrast images with bartorch.learning.ComplexNet, and balance the contrasts by whitening.

  • Train it on patches drawn by torchio, with augmentations that preserve the complex MR signal.

  • Apply it to a whole volume with bartorch.learning.Patchwise, and average the seams out with bartorch.learning.moments().

  • Compare the size of spatial and spatiotemporal networks.

Multi-contrast complex volume#

Your data are three spin-echo contrasts of a BrainWeb subject [1] on a \(64^3\) grid: \(T_1\)-weighted (TR 600 ms, TE 12 ms), \(T_2\)-weighted (TR 4000 ms, TE 100 ms) and proton-density-weighted (TR 4000 ms, TE 12 ms), each with its own smooth background phase. They come as one (3, z, y, x) complex tensor; how they are simulated is hidden. You train on subject 0 and test on subject 4, whose volume you corrupt with complex Gaussian noise of 6 per cent of each contrast’s peak, an SNR typical of a fast high-resolution scan.

The network#

UNet is a residual U-Net whose last convolution starts at zero, so the untrained network returns its input. A U-Net works on real channels, so you wrap it in ComplexNet, which lays the three complex contrasts out as six real channels – the real parts, then the imaginary parts – and lays the output back out as complex contrasts.

With normalize="whiten", ComplexNet subtracts each channel’s mean and multiplies by the inverse square root of the channels’ covariance before the call, and undoes both after it. The network then sees uncorrelated channels of unit variance, whatever the relative energy of the contrasts.

net = learning.UNet(6, spatial=3, widths=(8, 16, 32))
denoiser = learning.ComplexNet(net, spatial=3, channels=1, normalize="whiten")
weights = sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters())
print(f"{weights} weights, {4 * weights / 1e6:.2f} MB in single precision")

with torch.no_grad():
    identity = torch.allclose(denoiser(noisy[None])[0], noisy, atol=1e-5)

Patches and augmentation#

torchio holds the training volume as a torchio.ScalarImage of real channels (as_real()) and draws \(32^3\) patches from it through a torchio.Queue.

Choose augmentations that turn one MR image into another the acquisition could have produced: a flip, and RandomGain, a receiver gain and global phase shared by the contrasts. An intensity transform applied to the real and imaginary channels separately, such as a gamma correction, would produce a signal no acquisition can. The global phase varies over a limited range here: a network trained over every phase has to learn to commute with a rotation of its real and imaginary channels, which takes more training than this tour runs.

volume = learning.as_real(train_volume).flatten(0, 1)
subject = tio.Subject(image=tio.ScalarImage(tensor=volume))
augment = tio.Compose(
    [
        tio.RandomFlip(axes=(0, 1, 2)),
        learning.RandomGain(phase=0.3, log_scale=0.2),
    ]
)
queue = tio.Queue(
    tio.SubjectsDataset([subject], transform=augment),
    max_length=64,
    samples_per_volume=32,
    sampler=tio.UniformSampler(PATCH),
    num_workers=0,
)

Each patch becomes a training pair when you add a new draw of noise to it, so the network sees a different noise realization at every epoch. The test volume serves as the validation pair.

def pairs(patches):

Patchwise inference#

Patchwise keeps the volume in host memory, sends it to the network’s device a few patches at a time, runs the network there in mixed precision, and assembles the result on the host. On a GPU only the network and one group of batch patches are resident – two groups at inference, where the copies of one group overlap the computation on the other. That is how you denoise a volume larger than the GPU memory, such as a whole-brain fingerprinting series on a scanner’s 16 GB GPU, with a network trained on patches of it. Here, on the host, the whole volume is small enough to denoise in one call too, and that is your reference.

A U-Net is not translation invariant at a patch boundary: its receptive field extends past the patch, where it sees zeros rather than the neighbouring voxels. On a fixed grid of patches the errors fall along the same planes every time. With shift=True the grid is offset at random at every call, and averaging a few calls with moments() spreads the boundary errors across the volume.

grid = (PATCH,) * 3
whole_net = learning.ComplexNet(net, spatial=3, channels=1, normalize="whiten")
fixed = learning.ComplexNet(
    learning.Patchwise(net, grid, shift=False),
    spatial=3,
    channels=1,
    normalize="whiten",
)
shifted = learning.ComplexNet(
    learning.Patchwise(net, grid, shift=True),
    spatial=3,
    channels=1,
    normalize="whiten",
)

with torch.no_grad():
    whole = whole_net(noisy[None])[0]
    on_grid = fixed(noisy[None])[0]
    averaged, spread = learning.moments(
        lambda: shifted(noisy[None])[0],
        samples=8,
    )

The network, 0.14 million weights trained on patches of one head, reduces the relative error of the other head’s volume from about 0.19 to about 0.11, and in the sagittal \(T_2\)-weighted slice the corpus callosum and the cortical folding stay sharp. A real training set and a wider network remove more.

The departure of the fixed grid from the whole-volume result lies on the planes between patches – the seams – and on the same planes at every call. A shifted grid covers the volume with one more patch along each axis and so has more boundaries, including ones at the edges of the volume, and in norm the average of eight shifted calls departs further from the whole-volume result than the fixed grid does. The average has no planes, however: the departure is spread over the volume instead of concentrated where it would read as an anatomical edge. Inside an iteration, which applies the denoiser once per step, one shifted grid per call is enough, because no plane receives the boundary error at every step. The variance moments() returns is a map of how much the result depends on where the patches fall, one of the spreads of Uncertainty estimation.

Size of the network#

The weights set the storage footprint and, with the patch size, the memory of a call. The default widths of UNet, (32, 64, 128, 256), give a 3D network of a few million weights.

For a series of frames – a cine, a functional run – you pass frames=True. The network then convolves each frame spatially and the frames with a separate one-dimensional convolution, and never downsamples the frame axis; periodic=True pads that axis circularly, which suits a cardiac cycle. The factorization costs few weights beyond the spatial network.

candidates = {
    "3D, widths (8, 16, 32)": net,
    "3D, default widths": learning.UNet(6, spatial=3),
    "3D + frames, default widths": learning.UNet(
        6,
        spatial=3,
        frames=True,
        periodic=True,
    ),
}

Exercise: random patch grids#

Averaging more shifted calls costs one network pass each. How far from the whole-volume result is the average of 2, 4, 8 and 16 calls? moments() needs at least two, since it also returns a variance. No training is involved:

counts = (2, 4, 8, 16)
departures = []
with torch.no_grad():
    for count in counts:
        mean, _ = learning.moments(
            lambda: shifted(noisy[None])[0],
            samples=count,
        )
        departures.append(float((mean - whole).norm() / whole.norm()))

The departure decreases with the number of calls averaged, slowly, and stays above that of the fixed grid at every count tried. What averaging shifted grids buys is the distribution of the boundary error, off fixed planes, not a result closer to the whole-volume one.
