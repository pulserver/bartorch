Dynamic golden-angle radial MRI#

In this tour you reconstruct a dynamic contrast-enhanced series from one continuous golden-angle radial acquisition, which you cut into frames of thirteen spokes each after the scan. On its own, each frame is undersampled about fifteenfold, and its gridding reconstruction is dominated by streaks. Consecutive frames, however, show the same anatomy and differ by a slow change of contrast, so you reconstruct all frames jointly with a penalty on the temporal variation of the series. You compare frame-by-frame gridding with that joint reconstruction on the images, on an x-t profile, and on the time-intensity curve a perfusion analysis would read.

The acquisition uses the golden angle [1]: each spoke is rotated from the previous one by \(180^\circ / \phi \approx 111.25^\circ\), with \(\phi\) the golden ratio, so any block of consecutive spokes covers k-space approximately uniformly, independent of its length and starting point. You therefore acquire without interruption and choose the temporal resolution at reconstruction: fewer spokes per frame give finer temporal resolution and stronger streaks. Combined with parallel imaging and a sparsity penalty along time, this is GRASP [2].

The encoding is the radial SENSE of lesson 8 with a frame axis added: the image is (frames, y, x), the trajectory indexes frames as well as spokes, and all frames share the coil sensitivities.

Prerequisites: lessons 7 and 8, 7. Basics of non-Cartesian imaging and 8. Radial SENSE.

Learning objectives

  • Cut a continuous golden-angle acquisition into frames after the fact.

  • Build an encoding whose image and trajectory carry a frame axis.

  • Regularize along time with a total-variation term over the frame axis.

  • Compare frame-by-frame gridding with the joint reconstruction on the images, on an x-t profile and on the time-intensity curve of a region.

Contrast-enhanced series#

The object is the BrainWeb slice [3] of the course lessons with a bolus of contrast agent passing through it. A gamma-variate curve gives the first-pass concentration over time, and each tissue enhances in proportion to its blood volume: strongly in grey matter, weakly in white matter, not at all in cerebrospinal fluid. The series is smooth in time and has the same anatomy in every frame; the temporal penalty below relies on both properties. The code that builds it is hidden.

FRAMES = 16
SPOKES = 13  # per frame

# Relative enhancement at the peak of the bolus, per tissue class.
CLASSES = {"grey matter": ("GM", 0.8), "white matter": ("WM", 0.25)}

The cortex enhances most at the peak of the first pass, in frame 4, and is close to its baseline again in the last frame.

Acquisition#

You generate FRAMES * SPOKES spokes as one golden-angle trajectory and reshape it, so that the first axis indexes frames and the second the spokes within a frame. A fully sampled frame of 128 readout points needs about \(\pi/2 \times 128 \approx 201\) spokes, so thirteen undersample each frame about fifteenfold. The coils are BART’s analytical eight-channel head coil, normalized as in lesson 2, and the noise has the variance of the Cartesian lessons, \(10^{-4}\) per sample.

import torch

import bartorch
import bartorch.tools as bt

from bartorch import linop, optim, priors

COILS = 8

trajectory = bt.traj(
    readout=SIZE,
    spokes=FRAMES * SPOKES,
    radial=True,
    golden=True,
)
trajectory = trajectory.reshape(FRAMES, SPOKES, SIZE, 3)

sensitivities = bt.coils(t=bt.grid(D=(SIZE, SIZE, 1)), n=COILS)[:, 0]
sensitivities /= bartorch.rss(sensitivities, axes=(0,), keepdim=True)

A = linop.NoncartesianSense(
    sensitivities,
    (FRAMES, SIZE, SIZE),
    traj=trajectory,
)
measured = bt.noise(A(series), n=1e-4, s=3)

print(f"{A.ishape} -> {A.oshape}")
print(A.plan)

(16, 128, 128) -> (8, 16, 13, 128)
Plan(transform=nufft, items=16, image=sensitivities, normal=kernel, coil_batch=1, streamed=coils, executor=slab)

The image varies along the frame axis, so the operator holds one NUFFT per frame and applies them all inside the same loop over the coils; plan.items is the number of frames it carries.

Gridding and regularized reconstruction#

The first treats the frames as independent: the adjoint of the encoding applied to density-compensated samples, which is gridding of thirteen spokes per frame, with the coils combined by their sensitivities. The density compensation is the ramp \(|k|\), floored at a quarter of its maximum.

The second solves for the whole series at once, with a total-variation penalty along the frame axis: the solution is the series consistent with all the data that has the least total variation from frame to frame. Each frame has different spokes and therefore a different streak pattern, which has a large temporal total variation and is suppressed; the anatomy is the same in every frame and is retained.

density = torch.linalg.norm(trajectory.real[..., :2], dim=-1).clamp(min=0.25)
gridded = A.H(measured * density.to(torch.complex64))

data = measured / optim.data_scaling(measured[..., None], A=A)
temporal_tv = priors.TotalVariation(axes=(-3,), weight=0.02)
temporal = optim.ADMM(temporal_tv, maxiter=30)(data, A)

for name, volume in (("gridding", gridded), ("temporal TV", temporal)):
    nrmse = bt.nrmse(series.abs(), volume, scaled=True)
    print(f"{name:>12}: NRMSE over all frames {nrmse:.3f}")

   gridding: NRMSE over all frames 0.449
temporal TV: NRMSE over all frames 0.126

The frame axis is -3, the one in front of the two spatial axes. Given (-1, -2) the same term would penalize the spatial gradient instead, and given all three it would penalize both: the axes a term acts on are the whole difference between a spatial and a temporal regularizer.

In the gridded frame the streaks of thirteen spokes dominate, and only the ventricles and the outline of the head are recognizable. The joint reconstruction recovers the anatomy and the enhanced cortex. Its residual error is small and concentrated at the tissue boundaries, which carry the high spatial frequencies each frame samples most sparsely.

An x-t profile, one line of the image plotted against time, shows the time axis directly. The line below runs left to right through the ventricles and the grey matter on either side.

In the reference profile the grey matter brightens and fades over a few frames, while cerebrospinal fluid and the scalp stay constant. Gridding shows the same enhancement under a different streak pattern in every frame, so the profile varies from frame to frame where the object is constant. Temporal total variation removes that variation and retains the time course.

Time-intensity curves#

A perfusion study reports the mean signal in a region as a function of time, so you compare the two reconstructions on that curve too, in the grey matter where the enhancement is strongest.

region = memberships[CLASS["GM"]] > 0.6

curves = {
    "reference": series.abs(),
    "gridding": scaled(gridded, series),
    "temporal TV": scaled(temporal, series),
}
truth = curves["reference"][:, region].mean(-1)
for name in ("gridding", "temporal TV"):
    enhancement = curves[name][:, region].mean(-1)
    error = float((enhancement - truth).norm() / truth.norm())
    print(f"{name:>12}: curve NRMSE {error:.3f}")

   gridding: curve NRMSE 0.153
temporal TV: curve NRMSE 0.039

Averaged over the grey matter the streaks largely cancel, and gridding reproduces the shape of the curve but not its level: part of each frame’s signal is spread into streaks across the field of view, outside the region. The joint reconstruction follows the reference closely, with the peak attenuated and the late frames slightly raised. A temporal total-variation penalty attenuates a change that lasts only a few frames, and a larger weight trades more of the peak for less noise.

Exercise: retrospective frame length#

Because the spokes follow the golden angle, the same data can be regrouped into longer frames. Join two or four frames into one, grid each, and compare with the reference averaged over the same frames:

for join in (1, 2, 4):
    frames = FRAMES // join
    traj_j = trajectory.reshape(frames, join * SPOKES, SIZE, 3)
    data_j = measured.reshape(COILS, frames, join * SPOKES, SIZE)
    A_j = linop.NoncartesianSense(
        sensitivities,
        (frames, SIZE, SIZE),
        traj=traj_j,
    )
    k = torch.linalg.norm(traj_j.real[..., :2], dim=-1).clamp(min=0.25)
    gridded_j = A_j.H(data_j * k.to(torch.complex64))
    reference_j = series.abs().reshape(frames, join, SIZE, SIZE).mean(1)
    nrmse = bt.nrmse(reference_j, gridded_j, scaled=True)
    print(f"{join * SPOKES} spokes, {frames:2d} frames: NRMSE {nrmse:.3f}")

13 spokes, 16 frames: NRMSE 0.449
26 spokes,  8 frames: NRMSE 0.295
52 spokes,  4 frames: NRMSE 0.202

The NRMSE falls with the frame length, at the cost of temporal resolution: with 52 spokes per frame the rise of the bolus, frames 0 to 4, spans two frames. Repeat the comparison with the joint reconstruction, or vary the weight of the temporal term and observe the attenuation of the peak.

As a spec#

What this tour built, stated the way you would ask an agent for it:

Reconstruct a dynamic 2D series of 16 frames, 128 x 128, from one
golden-angle radial acquisition of 16 * 13 spokes (bt.traj with
golden=True, reshaped to (frames, spokes, readout, 3)), 8 coils, with
bartorch. Encode with linop.NoncartesianSense over (frames, y, x).
Reconstruct (a) frame by frame, as the adjoint of density-compensated
data (|k| floored at 0.25), and (b) jointly, with optim.ADMM (30
iterations) and priors.TotalVariation over the frame axis (-3), weight
0.02, on data divided by optim.data_scaling. Report NRMSE over all
frames and of the mean grey-matter time-intensity curve.
